Hard drive disposal in Santa Clara County: your options

Old drives pile up in every office and garage. Here's what California law says about getting rid of them, the free options around Santa Clara County, and when it's worth paying for certified destruction.

UPDATED: OCTOBER 1, 2026 · BY AIDEN RANGEL, FOUNDER

First: they can't go in the trash

California classifies computers, hard drives and most electronics as universal waste, a category of hazardous waste. Since 2006 that rule applies to households as well as businesses: e-waste has to go to an authorized collector or recycler, not the garbage bin.

Businesses have a second obligation. California law requires businesses to take reasonable steps to destroy customer records containing personal information — including by erasing — before disposing of them. Recycling a drive isn't the same as destroying the data on it.

Your options, from free to fully documented

Check your city's website for current e-waste drop-off locations and collection dates — programs and schedules change.

Doing it yourself

For a personal drive, wipe it with your computer's built-in reset before recycling. If the drive is dead or you'd rather not trust software, physical damage works for spinning hard drives: several holes drilled clean through the platters will stop casual recovery. SSDs are harder — the data lives on small chips that each have to be destroyed.

Wear eye protection, and don't try to open, crush or burn drives — platters can shatter, and batteries in laptops are a real fire risk.

When a certified service makes sense

  • The drives hold business, client, patient or financial data.
  • You need proof for an auditor, insurer, client contract or regulator.
  • You have more than a handful of drives — or failed drives that can't be wiped.
